Audience: an aerobiology or environmental monitoring team reviewing an outdoor air sample (for example from a cyclone or filter air sampler).
Emphasise: what the air contains and where it probably came from, using the composition results: the split between bacteria, fungi, plants (pollen) and animals, and what the main groups suggest about sources such as soil, vegetation and crops, water or the sea, or people and animals; airborne plant pathogens of crops and trees, with the strength of evidence for each; allergenic fungal spores and pollen; organisms of human health relevance (for example Legionella, Aspergillus fumigatus or Coxiella burnetii) and priority resistance genes, with the strength of evidence.
Data quality matters more than usual: air is a low-biomass sample, so read counts are often small, and DNA from extraction kits, reagents and handling can make up much of it. Say which dominant organisms are also common contaminants (they may still be genuine; several are true air and leaf-surface bacteria), that results should be compared with negative controls and field blanks, and whether sequencing depth was enough. Mention the sampling conditions (date, time, temperature, humidity) where recorded.
Do not describe read counts as concentrations or exposure levels, and do not imply that DNA shows viable spores, cells or pollen.
